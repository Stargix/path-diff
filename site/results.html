Evaluation details

We compare trained controllers, so differences in performance cannot be attributed to architecture alone. We include every attempt and report results by posture and requested speed. Each controller was evaluated in simulation with three online training runs.

Metrics and experimental scope

Original task pass
At first arrival, we check the robot’s position, heading, height and mean speed over the route. Height error along the route must also average at most 4 cm, excluding ±0.25 m around posture changes. A pass does not mean the robot reached every requested posture.
Tracking-gated pass
To pass, the robot must also stay close to the path: its root mean square sideways error over the active episode must be at most 10 cm.
Arrival and timing
For timing, we use the first arrival recorded by the task, not the separate measure of how far the robot has progressed along the route. We record both. Waiting at the endpoint cannot improve the score. The tolerance is 0.08 m/s at every requested speed, so it allows a larger relative error at lower speeds. It is not a strict deadline.
Active tracking errors
For each episode, we measure sideways tracking error (RMSE) and height error (MAE) over time, then average across runs. We include motion from startup until arrival, failure or timeout. A robot that fails early can therefore have a small error over the short distance it covered. These scores include drift while the controller settles.
All-section diagnostic
We also check whether height error averages at most 4 cm in every section, including transitions. We use the first recorded passage at each centimetre, excluding the first 4 cm and last 15 cm. Logging starts after the first step, so we allow one missing starting point. A gap elsewhere in the scored interval means failure; we do not fill in missing heights. This additional check has not been independently validated.
Height-response plots
At each distance along the path, we first average routes for each trained policy, then average across policies with data there. Shading shows their range, not a confidence interval. Fewer runs may contribute near the end. We show base-family routes at 0.25 m/s for single changes and 0.35 m/s for repeated changes. Individual runs can differ from the mean.
What is not measured yet
These plots cannot tell us whether motion is smooth or safe, whether the robot can hold intermediate heights, or whether modelling multiple behaviours explains a result. That requires comparing the requested change with the robot’s motion and distinguishing completed transitions from late, partial or missed ones.
Different systems and support
The hierarchy selects an expert at 5 Hz. Path Diff and Gaussian BC + PPO replan at 12.5 Hz; all apply joint targets at 50 Hz. The methods differ in expert access, training data and training procedure. We use previously selected checkpoints for diffusion and the hierarchy, and final checkpoints for Gaussian. The three Path Diff runs start from the same offline policy.
Separate sources of evidence
We evaluated imitation, refinement and the timing input in separate experiments, so we do not combine those results with the main comparison. The videos show new runs of the controllers, not replays of the runs behind the tables.

Reference: PPO without BC initialization

We also tested three Gaussian PPO runs trained with the same refinement setup but starting from random weights. They still use normalization statistics from demonstrations. Their failure here does not mean that reinforcement learning from scratch cannot solve the task.
